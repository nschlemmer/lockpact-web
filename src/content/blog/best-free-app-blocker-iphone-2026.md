## The Free Tier Problem

**Most "free" screen time apps are actually paywalls.**

They offer a limited free version to hook you, then charge $80–$150/year for the features that actually work. AppBlock does this. Opal does this. Freedom does this.

There's nothing dishonest about it. Free tier = marketing. But if you're looking for a screen time solution that doesn't cost money, most of the big names will disappoint you.

Here are 8 apps worth knowing, what's actually free in each one, and where the free tier is a trial in disguise.

## 1. LockPact

**Price:** Free, forever
**What's Free:** Everything — partner pairing, unlock requests, bypass detection, mutual locking
**The Catch:** You need another person

LockPact is built around one idea: you can't do this alone. So don't try.

You pair with someone you trust (a partner, roommate, sibling, or friend). You each select apps to block. When you want to unlock an app, your partner has to approve it. If you bypass (Apple lets you), your partner gets alerted immediately.

It's free because the enforcement mechanism is the relationship, not the software. You don't pay for premium features. Everything that exists is free.

**The social cost is the feature.** Texting your partner "Can you unlock Instagram?" is uncomfortable. So you don't do it unless you really need it. And you probably don't.

**Best for:** Two people who trust each other and want accountability that actually works.

**Verdict:** The only free app that solves the real problem. Requires buy-in from another person. Worth the setup.


## 2. One Sec

**Price:** Free with optional $5.99/month premium
**What's Free:** App blocking with customizable "friction" messages
**The Catch:** Less powerful blocking than enterprise apps; designed for "awareness" not "prevention"

One Sec is built on [science about habit formation](https://www.apa.org/science/about/psa/habit-formation). It doesn't try to forcibly prevent you from opening apps. Instead, it makes you *aware* of when you're about to open them.

You set up a custom message that appears before you can open Instagram: "Do you actually want to open this, or are you bored?" If you're honest with yourself, you'll sometimes close the app and do something else.

It's genuinely clever. And the free tier is full-featured.

The catch: this only works if you're already motivated to change. If you're not, you'll just read the message and open Instagram anyway. It's a tool for people already trying, not for people who don't think they have a problem.

Surprisingly, it's one of the few apps where [users actually report sustained behavior change](https://www.reddit.com/r/nosurf/). Maybe because it's not punitive. It's just... honest.

**Best for:** People who don't have severe addictions, but want to be more mindful of their phone use. People who can benefit from a pause before compulsive behavior.

**Verdict:** Excellent free app if you're already leaning toward changing. Won't save you if you're not. We go deeper on the friction-vs-accountability tradeoff in [One Sec vs. LockPact](/blog/onesec-vs-lockpact/).


## 3. ScreenZen

**Price:** Free (no paid tier currently offered)
**What's Free:** Friction delays before opening chosen apps, plus optional streak-sharing with friends
**The Catch:** No partner approval step — the "accountability" is social visibility, not a lock someone else controls

ScreenZen sits between One Sec and LockPact conceptually. Like One Sec, it uses friction — a short delay and a prompt — rather than a hard block. Like LockPact, it adds a social layer: you can share your streaks with friends, so slipping up is at least visible to someone.

The gap is that visibility isn't the same as control. Nobody has to approve anything before you open the app. A friend can see you broke your streak after the fact, but nobody could have stopped you in the moment. That's a meaningfully weaker mechanism than a partner who holds the actual key.

**Best for:** People who want light social pressure without asking a friend to actively gatekeep their apps.

**Verdict:** A reasonable middle ground if a full partner lock feels like too much commitment. See the full breakdown in [ScreenZen vs. LockPact](/blog/screenzen-vs-lockpact/).


## 4. Opal

**Price:** Free with $9.99/month premium ($100/year)
**What's Free:** Basic app blocking, no bypass protection, limited features
**The Catch:** Everything worth paying for costs money; free tier feels like a trial

We covered Opal in depth in [Opal vs. LockPact](/blog/opal-vs-lockpact), so let's keep this brief.

Opal's free tier lets you block apps. But the real features — Deep Focus (bypass-resistant locking), buddy notifications, Mac support — are locked behind the $100/year paywall.

The free tier exists to let you experience Opal without paying. Once you're hooked, the paywall appears. This is standard SaaS strategy, and it's honest marketing. But it's not a free app. It's a paid app with a free trial.

**Best for:** People who can afford $100/year and want the best-designed solo blocker. Not best for people looking for actual free tools.

**Verdict:** Free tier is a trial, not a product. The real app costs money.


## 5. AppBlock

**Price:** Free with premium unlock
**What's Free:** Scheduled blocking, location-based rules, basic Pomodoro timers
**The Catch:** The features that make it hard to cheat — Strict Mode, uninstall protection — sit behind the paid tier

AppBlock is the most feature-rich solo blocker on this list. If you want granular control — website blocking, location rules, custom schedules — the free tier already does more than most competitors' paid tiers.

The tradeoff: AppBlock's free version is honest-system, not hard-enforced. You can turn it off. Strict Mode, which makes that harder, is the paid upgrade. So the free tier is genuinely useful, but the version that resists your own willpower failure isn't free.

**Best for:** People who want fine-grained solo control and don't mind paying eventually for real enforcement.

**Verdict:** Best free feature set on this list for a solo tool. The real accountability layer is still a paid add-on — or a partner. Full comparison: [AppBlock vs. LockPact](/blog/appblock-vs-lockpact/).


## 6. Freedom

**Price:** ~$40–130/year depending on device count, with a limited free trial
**What's Free:** 7 blocking sessions total, not per month — after that, it's a paywall
**The Catch:** The free tier isn't really "free," it's a sample

Freedom's strength is cross-device blocking — one schedule that blocks apps and websites across your phone, laptop, and tablet simultaneously. For people who lose focus by hopping from phone to browser, that's genuinely useful.

But the free tier is the thinnest on this list. Seven sessions, ever, not per month. After that you're paying a subscription that scales with how many devices you want covered.

**Best for:** People who need blocking to follow them across a laptop and a phone, and are willing to pay for it.

**Verdict:** Strong tool, weakest "free" claim here. Budget for the subscription if you go this route. Full comparison: [Freedom vs. LockPact](/blog/freedom-vs-lockpact/).


## 7. Friend Controls

**Price:** Free
**What's Free:** A friend can remotely manage your app blocks
**The Catch:** Smaller, newer app — fewer reviews, less track record than the bigger names on this list

Friend Controls is conceptually the closest thing to LockPact on this list: someone else manages your blocks instead of you. The core mechanic — hand a friend the ability to lock and unlock your apps — is the same accountability principle this entire list keeps coming back to.

The difference is maturity and scope. LockPact is built specifically around *mutual* locking (you each hold the other's key, so it's not one-directional), plus bypass detection that tells your partner if you turn Screen Time off yourself. Friend Controls is worth trying if you want a lighter version of the same idea. For the full picture of every app built around a friend or partner holding the key, see our dedicated roundup of [apps that let a friend control your screen time](/blog/apps-that-let-a-friend-control-your-screen-time/).

**Best for:** People who want to test whether "someone else holds the key" works for them, without a mutual commitment.

**Verdict:** A legitimate free option in the same category as LockPact — worth comparing directly if the mutual, bypass-detecting version feels like more than you need.


## 8. Cold Turkey

**Price:** Free basic blocking; Cold Turkey Pro is $39 one-time for Mac/Windows
**What's Free:** Website and app blocking on desktop, with a "Frozen Turkey" mode that can't be disabled once started
**The Catch:** It's a desktop tool first — the mobile story is much thinner than the Mac/Windows product

Worth including here mainly for honesty: if you searched "app blocker iPhone" and landed on Cold Turkey, you should know its real strength is Mac and Windows, not iOS. The Frozen Turkey mode — a block you genuinely cannot turn off until the timer runs out — is one of the most aggressive enforcement mechanisms available, and it's free at the basic tier.

**Best for:** Deadline-driven desktop work where you need blocking that's impossible to override, not phone-first accountability.

**Verdict:** The most hardcore free blocking on this list, but it's solving a desktop problem. Full comparison: [Cold Turkey vs. LockPact](/blog/coldturkey-vs-lockpact/).


## What Reddit Recommends

Search r/nosurf, r/productivity, or r/getdisciplined for "best app blocker iPhone" and a handful of names come up repeatedly, based on browsing those threads directly rather than any formal survey. Opal and One Sec get mentioned most often in our own reading — Opal from people willing to pay for polish, One Sec from people who want something free and are already trying to change. Cold Turkey comes up often too, usually with someone clarifying in the replies that it's really a desktop tool. AppBlock tends to get recommended by people specifically after granular scheduling rules.

One pattern was consistent across the threads we looked at: recommendations for a tool that involves another person were rare. Most of Reddit's advice in this space is solo-tool advice, because that's what most of these apps are. And a recurring complaint shows up in a lot of "what app blocker actually worked for you" threads — people report initial success, then admit to quietly turning the blocker off a few weeks later, because nothing stopped them except themselves.


## The Honest Truth About Free Blockers

[Research on behavior change](https://www.apa.org/science/about/psa/social-influence) is clear: accountability beats blockers. Every time.

A free friction tool is better than nothing. But it's weaker than a locked system. And a locked system (that you can override) is weaker than a person who cares about your commitment.

So if your goal is to *actually change* your phone habits, free isn't the right variable. Free doesn't matter. What matters is accountability.

That's why LockPact works for free. It's not free as a gimmick. It's free because it doesn't need to charge you. The real enforcement mechanism is your partner.

And that's why Opal charges money. Not because blocking is expensive to build. But because the app is trying to replace something that can't be replaced: another person's accountability.

Pick the right tool for what you actually need. Not the cheapest one.
