Your Screen Time passcode is the one thing standing between you and every limit you've set, and in most households the person who knows it is the person it was meant to restrain. This guide covers Apple's own steps to **change it, reset it if you forgot it, and turn it off**, then makes the case for handing it to someone else. Everything here is free and built into your iPhone. If you want the mutual version, [LockPact](/) is a free iOS app where your partner approves your unlocks and you approve theirs.

The steps below are written for iOS 27, which Apple released on September 14, 2026. Apple's current support pages don't list differences for earlier versions, so if your menus look different, update first.

## How do you change your Screen Time passcode?

Open **Settings**, tap **Screen Time**, tap **Manage Screen Time**, then tap **Change Screen Time Passcode** and tap it again to confirm. If prompted, enter the Apple Account email and password used when the passcode was first set up, then enter a new passcode twice.

That's the whole process, and it takes under a minute. Two details are worth knowing. First, Apple's page says you may be asked for the Apple Account details, and that account can differ from the one signed in on your phone. Second, if you use Share Across Devices, a changed passcode updates your other devices automatically.

## What if you forgot your Screen Time passcode?

Follow the same path as above: **Settings → Screen Time → Manage Screen Time → Change Screen Time Passcode**, tap it again, and sign in with the Apple Account you used for Screen Time Passcode Recovery. Apple says that if you don't have that account, you can't remove or reset the passcode, and Apple can't recover it for you.

This is why recovery matters. When you first set a passcode, Apple offers an optional recovery step where you add an Apple Account. If you have more than one Apple Account, use the one you entered at setup, not necessarily the one on the phone. If the reset fails, Apple's first suggestion is to check you're using exactly that email and password.

One more thing: this guide sticks to Apple's route only. We don't cover third-party unlock tools or workarounds. If Apple's own recovery path doesn't work, Apple Support is the place to go.

## How do you turn off the Screen Time passcode?

Open **Settings**, tap **Screen Time**, scroll down and tap **Manage Screen Time**, then tap **Change Screen Time Passcode**. Tap **Turn Off Screen Time Passcode** and enter your current passcode to confirm. You need to know the code to remove it, which is exactly why the code matters.

Turning it off removes the passcode, not Screen Time itself, and it isn't something to do casually. Your limits and schedules remain, but anyone holding the phone can now change them without entering anything. If your goal is to stop overriding your own limits, turning the passcode off is the opposite of what you want.

## What does the passcode actually protect?

It's the code Apple asks for when someone opens your Screen Time restriction settings: your app limits, your schedules, and your content restrictions. That's the part Apple documents. What the passcode covers beyond that, and how it relates to the newer lock setting, is less clear-cut, so here's what we can and can't confirm.

You'll also see a menu item called **Lock Screen Time Settings**. It isn't a second passcode: in Apple's current documentation it's the path for setting the Screen Time passcode in the first place (**Settings → Screen Time → Manage Screen Time → Lock Screen Time Settings**, then a four-digit passcode entered twice). Apple says the passcode "must be entered before you can change any Screen Time settings." Third-party guides report that since iOS 26.4 the same passcode also gates switching off an app's Screen Time access and deleting the app; Apple's own pages don't spell that out, so test it on your phone. [Our deep dive](/blog/lock-screen-time-settings-partner-passcode/) covers it.

If you want the whole picture of what each built-in lock does and who can undo it, [how to lock (or block) apps on iPhone](/blog/how-to-lock-apps-on-iphone/) covers every one. What it doesn't protect is simple: it can't stop a determined phone owner. Apple gives third-party apps no way to prevent that, and there's no setting that makes it impossible. What a passcode does is make quitting slower, and, if it isn't yours, make it a conversation.

## Why shouldn't you be the one who holds it?

A passcode you know is a limit you can override at 11pm, when you're tired and your hand is already reaching. Every Screen Time limit has the same weakness: the person who set it can remove it. The passcode only moves the weak point back one step, to "do I remember the code?"

Handing it to someone else changes the question. Instead of "can I get around this," it becomes "am I willing to ask my partner, out loud, to let me back in?" For a lot of people that second question is the one that actually holds.

The practical setup is short. Have your partner or a friend you trust set the passcode on your phone, choose it themselves, and skip the optional Apple Account recovery step if that account is one you can get into, since a recovery path that leads back to you cancels the point. The [full walkthrough is here](/blog/lock-screen-time-settings-partner-passcode/), including the loopholes that still exist. And if you're deciding who to trust with it, [apps where a friend controls your Screen Time](/blog/apps-that-let-a-friend-control-your-screen-time/) compares the options, and [what to do when a partner bypasses a lock](/blog/what-to-do-when-partner-bypasses-lock/) covers the harder conversation if it happens.

Keep it adult-to-adult. This only works when you chose it. A passcode imposed on someone who didn't agree to it isn't accountability, and it's not what this page is about.

## What should you do before you change or hand off the code?

Do three quick things first. Write down which Apple Account you used for recovery, because that's the one Apple will ask for later. Decide whether the recovery step is a feature or a loophole for you: if you're keeping the passcode yourself, recovery is a safety net, and if someone else is holding it, recovery that leads back to you defeats the arrangement. Finally, agree out loud what the other person will say yes to, whether that's emergencies, work apps, or a set window each evening.

That last point is the one people skip. A passcode without an agreed rule turns into an argument the first time it's used.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Who should hold my Screen Time passcode?

Someone other than you: a partner or friend you trust to say no. If you know the code, you can change or remove it in a few taps, so it only works as a boundary when somebody else is the only one who holds it.

### How do I change my Screen Time passcode?

Open Settings, tap Screen Time, tap Manage Screen Time, then tap Change Screen Time Passcode twice. Enter the Apple Account details if asked, then type the new passcode and confirm it.

### What if I forgot my Screen Time passcode?

Use the same Change Screen Time Passcode path and sign in with the Apple Account you set up for Screen Time Passcode Recovery. Apple says that without that account you can't reset the passcode, and Apple can't provide it.

### Does a Screen Time passcode stop me bypassing my own limits?

Only if someone else holds it. Even then, a device owner can still get around any iPhone restriction. A passcode makes bypass slower and harder to do quietly; it doesn't make it impossible.
