## What Clearspace actually does

Clearspace is a screen time app built around a simple idea: make opening a distracting app cost a little friction, and give you data on how well you're sticking to your own limits.

The mechanics, per its current App Store listing:

- **App blocking and time budgets.** You pick apps to limit and set a usage budget — as tight as 1 minute or as loose as 10 minutes per session.
- **Forced pauses.** Before you can open a limited app, Clearspace inserts a pause, similar in spirit to one sec's breathing delay — a moment designed to interrupt the automatic reach for the app.
- **Schedules.** You can restrict access to certain apps during set hours (work, dinner, before bed).
- **Streaks and reports.** The app tracks how many days you've stayed under budget and shows usage reports over time.
- **Pushup-to-unlock.** One of Clearspace's more distinctive features: instead of just waiting out a timer, you can do a set of pushups (tracked via your phone's motion sensor) to earn extra time on a blocked app. It's a genuinely unusual mechanic — most competitors don't have anything like it.

None of this requires anyone else. Like most screen time apps, the default experience is entirely solo: you set the budget, you hit the pause, you decide whether to push through it.

## The accountability partner feature — a closer look

Clearspace markets a "Screen Time Accountability" feature, described in its own App Store listing as letting you "have trusted accountability partners help you stick to your goals." It also has a "teammates" concept where partners are notified when you exceed your budgeted time or pause your detox.

This is worth being precise about, because "accountability partner" is doing a lot of marketing work here. Based on how the feature is described, it functions as **visibility, not approval**: your partner or teammate gets notified when you go over budget or pause your goals — they can see it happened and, presumably, say something about it. What they can't do is stand between you and the app in the moment. There's no request-and-approve step where a partner has to actively unlock something before you get back in. You set your own budget, you can adjust your own budget, and the "accountability" is someone finding out after the fact, not someone holding a key before the fact.

That's the same shape as Opal's "Buddies" feature or ScreenZen's streak-sharing: a real, useful layer of social visibility, but structurally different from a partner who has to say yes before you get unlocked. Visibility changes behavior for some people — knowing someone will notice is a real deterrent. But it's a softer mechanism than an actual gate, because you're still the only one who can override your own limit in the moment it matters.

## Where Clearspace is genuinely good

**The pushup mechanic is a smart piece of friction design.** Making the override cost physical effort instead of just a tap is a more interesting idea than most competitors bother with. If you respond better to "do 20 pushups" than "wait 30 seconds," this is a real point in Clearspace's favor.

**It's a complete package for solo behavior change.** Budgets, pauses, schedules, streaks, and reports cover most of what a self-directed screen time app needs to do, and the reporting is genuinely useful for seeing patterns over time.

**The student free tier is a real value.** Full access at no cost for students is a meaningful discount most competitors don't match.

**Cross-platform.** iOS, Android, and a Chrome extension means Clearspace follows you across devices in a way an iOS-only app can't.

## Where Clearspace falls short

**The accountability feature doesn't gate anything.** This is the core limitation. If the actual problem is "I override my own limits the moment I'm tired or bored," a notification-after-the-fact doesn't change the moment of decision the way a partner who has to approve your unlock does.

**You're still the one adjusting your own budget.** Nothing stops you from opening Clearspace and loosening your own limit when the pause gets annoying. The system assumes you want to keep the restriction — same assumption Apple's native Screen Time makes, and the same one that fails for a lot of people under real conditions.

**Premium gates the features that matter most.** The free tier is a taste; the accountability layer, deeper reporting, and full customization sit behind the paywall.

## When Clearspace is actually the better pick

To be fair about this comparison, there are real situations where Clearspace is the smarter choice:

**You're on Android, or you switch between phone and laptop.** LockPact is iOS-only, built on Apple's Screen Time API. If a meaningful share of your distraction happens on Android or in a Chrome tab, Clearspace's cross-platform coverage does something LockPact can't.

**You want a solo tool with real structure, not a partner requirement.** LockPact's entire mechanism depends on having someone willing to hold your key. If that's not available to you right now, Clearspace's self-contained budgets, pauses, and reports are a complete, capable system on their own.

**You respond to physical friction over social friction.** The pushup mechanic is a genuinely different lever than "text your partner." Some people would rather do 20 pushups than have an uncomfortable conversation, and that's a legitimate preference.

**You want detailed usage analytics.** Clearspace's reporting is more built-out than LockPact's, which deliberately doesn't track or surface your usage patterns — LockPact only handles lock and unlock requests, not analytics.

If none of those describe you — you're iPhone-primary, you have a willing partner, and what you actually need is someone who can say no before you get back into the app, not after — the free, mutual option is hard to beat on the specific mechanism that matters.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is Clearspace worth paying for?

If you want cross-platform coverage, detailed reporting, and the pushup-unlock mechanic, the $44.99–$79.99/year Premium tier is reasonably priced against competitors like Opal. If what you actually need is someone else holding your key, no amount of Clearspace's Premium tier gets you there — its accountability feature is visibility, not approval.

### Does Clearspace's accountability partner feature actually stop you from using an app?

No. Based on its own description, a partner or teammate gets notified when you exceed your budget or pause your goals — they don't have to approve anything before you're back in the app. The block, and the ability to lift it, both stay with you.

### What's the pushup-to-unlock feature actually like?

Clearspace uses your phone's motion sensor to verify a set of pushups before granting extra time on a blocked app. It's a physical-effort alternative to just waiting out a timer or tapping through a friction screen — a genuinely distinctive mechanic among screen time apps.

### Is Clearspace better than Opal?

They're closer to each other than either is to a partner-accountability app — both are solo tools with an optional social-visibility layer, not partner approval. Clearspace is cheaper and cross-platform; Opal has a longer track record and a more built-out focus-session system. Neither one requires or involves anyone actively saying yes or no to your override.

### Is there a free version of Clearspace?

Yes, plus free access for verified students. The free tier covers core blocking and budgets; the accountability/teammates layer, deeper reports, and full customization require Premium.
