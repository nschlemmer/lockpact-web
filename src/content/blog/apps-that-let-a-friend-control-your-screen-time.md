Search "apps that let a friend control your screen time" and you'll find TikToks about it, a handful of raw App Store listings, and basically no one who's actually compared them. That's strange, because the category is real, it's growing, and the apps in it work nothing alike.

Apps that let a friend control your screen time are apps where someone else — a friend, partner, or designated "Controller" — has to approve unlocking a blocked app on your phone, not just you.

This is that comparison. Every app below, every price, every mechanic — checked against its live App Store listing or website this week, not recalled from memory. Where we couldn't confirm something, we say so.

## Why self-controlled blockers fail

Most screen time apps have the same design flaw: the person who needs the limit is also the person who can remove it.

You set a 30-minute Instagram limit. You hit it. You tap "Ignore Limit." Nobody stops you, because nobody else is involved. The app didn't fail — it did exactly what you told it to. You just told it something different an hour later, under different willpower conditions, and it listened to that version of you too.

Friction-based tools (one sec's breathing delay, ScreenZen's escalating pauses, Opal's Deep Focus mode) push back against this by making the override slower or more annoying. That helps — friction genuinely reduces impulsive opens. But it's still a fight between you and you. Add enough friction and a determined person routes around it; remove enough motivation and a tired person just waits it out.

The apps in this roundup take a different approach: they hand the override decision to somebody else.

## The partner-held-key model

The mechanic varies app to app, but the shared idea is simple: when you want past your limit, a real person — not a setting — decides whether you get through.

That changes the psychology in a way friction alone doesn't. It's not "am I strong enough to wait 10 seconds," it's "am I willing to ask my partner, at 11pm, why I need Instagram back." For a lot of people, the second question is the one that actually works.

Below are the apps we could verify are live, on iOS, and built around a friend or partner in the approval loop — plus where the popular "solo" tools fit for contrast.

## The roundup

### LockPact — mutual, not one-way

LockPact pairs two people for **mutual** accountability: you each pick apps to lock on your own phone, and only your partner can approve your unlock requests (and vice versa, if you've paired). There's no "controller" and "controlled" — both people hold a key to the other's lock. The app also detects if you disable Screen Time access to bypass it and notifies your partner. It's free, with no subscription — a one-time Support LockPact tip or a rewarded ad on unlock is how it sustains itself. iOS only.

### Friend Controls — you set the limit, friends approve going over it

[Friend Controls](https://friendcontrols.com) works differently than the name might suggest: you set your own daily limits per app, and when you hit them, you send a request to friends you've invited as "Controllers," who approve or deny it. Blocking a single app is free; blocking more than one requires a paid tier (weekly, monthly, and yearly plans ranging roughly $3–$60 depending on term, plus a $29.99 lifetime option, per its App Store listing). It supports multiple friends as approvers, not just one partner, and both your limits and your Controllers are self-selected. 4.7 stars, 569 ratings, iOS 17.4+, developed by Ryan Moazzami. No Android. We compare it to LockPact in detail [here](/blog/friend-controls-vs-lockpact/).

### FriendLock — earn time with a selfie, no approval involved

[FriendLock](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/friendlock-screentime-friends/id6743492236) is worth including because it's the app most likely to get confused with the "friend controls your screen time" category — and worth being precise about, because it isn't actually that. You pick apps to limit, then take a selfie with a friend to earn one hour of usable screen time (bankable up to three hours). There's no request, no approval, no friend saying yes or no — once you've earned the hour, it's yours to spend whenever you want. It's a social-proof nudge, not a gatekeeper. Free with an optional Pro upgrade ($1.99/month, $14.99/year, or $49.99 lifetime). 4.8 stars off a small base of 27 ratings, iOS 16+, developed by Mitness Limited. Full comparison [here](/blog/friendlock-vs-lockpact/).

### PeerPause — a "Time Buddy" for extension requests

[PeerPause](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/peerpause-ask-for-screen-time/id6754638744) blocks your chosen apps once a daily limit runs out, and getting more time means sending a request — with a reason — to a designated "Time Buddy" who approves or denies it. It also gates changes to your own limits behind buddy approval, which stops the classic move of just raising your own ceiling. Being the Time Buddy — the person receiving and approving requests, not the one being blocked — is free; a Pro subscription ($3.99/week, $5.99/month, or $24.99/year) is only required to create and manage your own limits. At v1.1.10 (early-mid September 2026), it's still a very new listing — 5 stars but only 2 ratings — so treat it as unproven at scale. iOS 18+.

### Screen Time Buddy - Focus — gamified ranks plus friend approval

[Screen Time Buddy](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/screen-time-buddy-control/id6535687314) layers a gamification system (daily "character ranks" from Eagle to Sloth based on usage) on top of a friend-approval mechanic for extension requests. Free with in-app purchases; paid tier is $24.99/month or $59.99/year. Rating has moved to 4.3 stars off 10 ratings (up from 9), iOS 17.6+. Note: we could not independently verify a fixed daily allowance of self-bypasses anywhere in its current App Store listing or on its own site — its site does reference a "Panic Button" for urgent access, but without a stated frequency limit, so we're not repeating a specific bypass-count claim here without a live source for it.

### Useless – Screen Time Control — the "Walk of Shame" feed

[Useless](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/useless-screen-time-control/id6755298184) blocks apps at your limit; asking for more means posting a photo explanation to friends' shared "Walk of Shame" feed. Its strictest tier needs a friend's approval or a 24-hour wait to loosen. 4.5 stars, 277 ratings, v1.2.7, iOS 18+. Free core; Pro is $3.99–$11.99/mo or $24.99–$49.99/yr.

### Snitch — a shared time "pot"

[Snitch](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/snitch-screen-time-control/id6753588362) puts you in an accountability group sharing one time "pot" — self-unlocking notifies everyone, and an optional mode requires group approval first. 4.1 stars, 46 ratings, v1.0.20, iOS 17+. Premium is $2.99 solo / $7.99 group (up to 5 people), or $69.99 lifetime.

### SelfBlock: Screen Time Buddy — a buddy, or a stranger, decides

[SelfBlock](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/selfblock-screen-time-blocker/id6757798472) — a different app from "Screen Time Buddy" above, similar name aside — sends extension requests to a designated buddy by push notification or Apple Watch; with no buddy set up, an anonymous community member decides instead. No ratings yet, v1.3.0, iOS 18.6+. Free core; Pro is $4.49/mo, $27.49/yr, or $82.99 lifetime.

### Screen Freedom — free, with an optional real veto

[Screen Freedom](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/screen-freedom/id1642737751) partners two people on shared goals; by default, self-unblocking just notifies your partner afterward. Its optional accountability-lock mode gives them a real veto instead — accept or deny before you're back in. 5.0 stars (4 ratings), v2.2.1, iOS 17+, and genuinely free, with no IAP at all.

## Apps where someone else holds the password

That's the literal shape of every app on this page: you set the block, and someone else — a friend, a group, a stranger in an app's own community, or a partner — has to say yes before you're back in. Useless, Snitch, SelfBlock, and Screen Freedom each take a different angle on that: a public shame feed, a shared time pool, an anonymous vote, or an optional veto. None hand the password back to you alone.

### For contrast: the self-controlled tools

Opal, one sec, and ScreenZen are excellent at what they do, but they're not in this category — they're solo friction tools. Opal's focus sessions and Deep Focus mode are things *you* start and stop (an optional "accountability buddy" only gets notified, they don't approve anything). one sec and ScreenZen slow down app opens with a pause or breathing prompt, but the decision to push through is still entirely yours. If you want a friend or partner in the actual approval loop, none of the three put one there. Worth flagging for 2026: ScreenZen's v4.0.0 update added a genuine settings-lock feature where an accountability partner can approve reopening your settings, and Jolt now offers a similar one-way "Accountability Partner" override mode — real additions, but both still gate one person's own settings, not a mutual, two-way lock.

## FAQ

### Can someone else control my Screen Time?

Not through Apple's own Screen Time settings — those are tied to your device and, for adults, only you can change them (or a Family Sharing organizer, for a child's device). What these apps do instead is build a request-and-approval layer on top: you configure the block yourself, but getting past it requires a real person, not just a passcode, to say yes. LockPact goes a step further and makes the lock itself mutual — your partner sets what's locked on your phone (and you set theirs), so it's not just approval, it's shared control from the start.

### Is there a free app where my friend approves my screen time?

Yes. LockPact is free with no subscription tier — the core mutual-lock-and-approve mechanic is the whole product, not a teaser for a paywall. Friend Controls, PeerPause, and Screen Time Buddy are also free to start but paywall meaningful parts of the experience (more than one blocked app, unlimited Time Buddy requests, or the app outright) behind a subscription.

### What's the difference between these apps and parental control apps?

Parental control apps (Covenant Eyes, Bark, Family Sharing screen time limits) are built for one person to monitor or restrict another — usually a parent and a child — with the watched party having little or no control back. Everything in this roundup is peer-to-peer: you choose to add a friend, partner, or Controller, and in LockPact's case, they're also locked by you in return. It's accountability between equals, not surveillance from above.

### Does bypassing these apps just mean turning off Screen Time in Settings?

Yes — for any app built on Apple's Screen Time API, which is all of these, the device owner can revoke access through Settings and a Face ID prompt in a few taps. Apple doesn't give third-party apps a way to prevent that; it's a platform-level limit, not a bug in any one app. What separates the apps here is whether they notice and tell your partner. LockPact detects the bypass and notifies your partner when it happens — the point isn't making bypass impossible, it's making it visible.

### Which one should I actually pick?

If you want genuinely mutual accountability — both people hold a real lock on the other, for free, forever — that's [LockPact](/). If you want to set your own limits and lean on a wider circle of friends for extensions rather than one partner, Friend Controls is closer to what you want, and it's worth reading our honest [Friend Controls vs. LockPact](/blog/friend-controls-vs-lockpact/) comparison first. If a lightweight social nudge (earn time with a selfie, no one gatekeeping you) sounds like enough friction, see our [FriendLock vs. LockPact](/blog/friendlock-vs-lockpact/) breakdown. And if none of these partner-based apps sound right and you just want the best solo blocker, we keep an updated list in [Best Free App Blockers for iPhone (2026)](/blog/best-free-app-blocker-iphone-2026/).

### Do I need my friend to have the same app installed?

Yes, for every app in this roundup — the friend, partner, Controller, or Time Buddy needs the app installed too, since the approval (or, for FriendLock, the selfie) happens inside it. None of these work by texting a screenshot or asking verbally; the request-and-response has to run through the app so it can actually re-lock or unlock the relevant apps on your device. Factor that into which one you pick: if the person you have in mind won't download another app, the choice narrows fast.

### What if my partner and I both want to be locked, not just one of us?

That's mutual accountability, and LockPact is the one built for it: pairing itself creates two locks, one per person, from the moment you pair. Friend Controls, PeerPause, and Screen Time Buddy are all built around one person setting limits and a separate friend or buddy approving overages — the friend isn't also locked by you in return unless you set up a second, mirrored account of their own. FriendLock doesn't lock anyone in the approval sense at all.

### Is there an app where someone else holds the password to my apps?

Yes — it's a growing pattern. Useless posts your override requests to a friend group's "Walk of Shame" feed. Snitch pools time with an accountability group. SelfBlock routes requests to a buddy, or an anonymous community member if you haven't set one up. Screen Freedom's optional accountability-lock mode hands your partner a real veto instead of just a notification. LockPact remains the only one where that password-holding runs in both directions.

### Which of these are mutual — both people locked?

Only LockPact. The four newer apps here — Useless, Snitch, SelfBlock, and Screen Freedom — all pair one person's limits with a separate friend, group, or buddy who approves overages, without that approver also being locked by you in return.

## How to actually choose

Start with who you have in mind, not which app has the most features. One dedicated partner who'll also let you lock their apps → LockPact. A friend group you'd rather spread the asking across → Friend Controls or Snitch's shared time pool. Someone you'd rather not put in a position to say no → FriendLock. A single buddy who's fine with an occasional approve/deny text-style prompt → PeerPause or Screen Time Buddy, with the gamification layer as a tiebreaker between those two. Comfortable with public exposure as the deterrent → Useless. No specific buddy in mind → SelfBlock's anonymous community mode. Want it free with zero IAP and an optional real veto instead of just a heads-up → Screen Freedom. All nine are real, live, and doing roughly what they claim — the differences that matter are who holds the power and what it costs to find out.
