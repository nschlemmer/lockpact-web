Every iPhone can lock or block an app four or five different ways, and all of them are free, built in, and honest about one limit: you can turn each one off yourself. This guide covers **every built-in method with exact steps and its real off-switch**, so you can pick the right one, then explains why they all fail the same way and what to do about it. If you want a lock that someone else has to approve, [LockPact](/) is a free iOS app where your partner decides when you get back in.

The steps are written for iOS 27, which Apple released on September 14, 2026. Apple's menu names for a few Screen Time items have moved around between versions, so where we couldn't confirm an exact label on Apple's current pages, we point you to the Screen Time menu and say so.

## How do you lock an app on iPhone?

Press and hold the app's icon on your Home Screen, tap **Require Face ID** (or Touch ID or Passcode, depending on your phone), then tap **Require Face ID** again and authenticate. From then on the app needs your face, finger, or passcode to open, and it relocks every time you leave it.

That's Apple's built-in app lock, and for most people it's all they mean by "lock apps." It protects the app from someone picking up your phone. It does not protect the app from you.

### What can't be locked this way?

Apple lists the exceptions: Calculator, Camera, Clock, Contacts, Find My, Maps, Shortcuts, and Settings can't use Require Face ID. If you wanted to lock Settings so nobody can change your Screen Time rules, this isn't the tool. A Screen Time passcode is.

### How do you hide an app?

Hiding goes one step further. Press and hold the icon, tap **Require Face ID**, tap **Hide and Require Face ID**, authenticate, then tap **Hide App**. The app disappears from your Home Screen and moves to a **Hidden** folder at the bottom of App Library, which also needs authentication to open. Only apps you downloaded can be hidden; apps that come installed with iOS 18 or later can't be.

**The off-switch:** press and hold the app (or open it from the Hidden folder), tap **Don't Require Face ID**, and authenticate. That's you, with your own face. Locked and hidden status is also device-specific, so it doesn't follow you to another iPhone.

## How do you block apps on iPhone with Screen Time?

Open **Settings → Screen Time**, then choose the tool that matches what you want: **App Limits** for a daily time budget on an app or category, **Downtime** for scheduled hours when most apps are off-limits, or **Content & Privacy Restrictions** for rules about what can be used at all. Apple calls these features "limits" and "restrictions"; we'll use its names here.

Each one does a different job, and each has the same weakness.

### App Limits

App Limits give an app or whole category a daily allowance. In Apple's Screen Time menu, tap **App Limits**, then **Add Limit**, pick the apps or categories, and set the time, with different times per day if you want. When time's up, the app is locked for the day. **The off-switch:** you can adjust or delete the limit from the same screen, unless a Screen Time passcode you don't know is in the way.

### Downtime

Downtime is a schedule. In **Settings → Screen Time**, tap **Downtime**, turn it on, and set the hours. During those hours only the apps you've allowed stay available. It suits evenings and sleep better than it suits focused work. **The off-switch:** the same Screen Time screen, so again it's whoever knows the passcode.

### Content & Privacy Restrictions

Under **Settings → Screen Time → Content & Privacy Restrictions**, you can restrict content, purchases, and which features and apps are allowed. It's the most thorough built-in option and the least convenient to configure. We haven't confirmed the exact toggles for iOS 27 on Apple's current pages, so open the menu on your phone and read the labels rather than trusting a screenshot from an older guide. **The off-switch:** flip it off, with the Screen Time passcode if one is set.

## How do you lock yourself into one app?

**Guided Access** is the odd one out: instead of locking apps away, it locks you *in* to one app. Go to **Settings → Accessibility → Guided Access**, turn it on, and set a passcode (Face ID or Touch ID is optional). Open the app, triple-click the side button, and tap **Start**. You can even disable touch on parts of the screen.

To end a session, triple-click the side button again (double-click on iOS 18 or earlier), authenticate with the Guided Access passcode or Face ID, and tap **End**. It's the right tool for handing your phone to someone for one app, or for a focused stretch of reading. It does nothing for the rest of your apps once the session ends. **The off-switch:** the Guided Access passcode, which you chose.

## Which built-in method should you use?

Here's the short comparison. Everything is free, because Apple includes all of it.

| Method | What it stops | Who can turn it off | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Require Face ID | Others opening one app | You, with Face ID | Free |
| Hide app | Others finding one app | You, with Face ID | Free |
| App Limits | Your own daily use of an app | Whoever knows the Screen Time passcode (you, by default) | Free |
| Downtime | Your use during set hours | Whoever knows the Screen Time passcode (you, by default) | Free |
| Content & Privacy Restrictions | Selected content and apps | Whoever knows the Screen Time passcode (you, by default) | Free |
| Guided Access | Leaving one app during a session | The Guided Access passcode you set | Free |

If your worry is other people on your phone, Require Face ID or hiding is the answer. If your worry is *yourself*, the Screen Time tools are the answer, and that's where the problem starts.

## Why does every built-in lock fail the same way?

Because you know the code. Require Face ID opens for your face. App Limits, Downtime, and Content & Privacy Restrictions all unlock with the Screen Time passcode, and by default you set that passcode. The person who needs the limit is the person who can remove it, usually at 11pm when you're tired and your thumb is already moving.

No setting changes that completely. A device owner can always get around an iPhone restriction, and anyone who tells you otherwise is overclaiming. What changes the outcome is who holds the code. If your partner or a friend you trust sets the Screen Time passcode and you don't know it, turning off a limit stops being a tap and becomes a conversation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I lock an app with Face ID on iPhone?

Press and hold the app icon on your Home Screen, tap Require Face ID, then tap it again and authenticate. To undo it, press and hold the icon and choose Don't Require Face ID. Some built-in apps, such as Settings and Camera, can't be locked.

### Can I lock the Settings app on iPhone?

Not with Require Face ID. Apple lists Settings, Camera, Calculator, Clock, Contacts, Find My, Maps and Shortcuts as apps that can't be locked that way. To protect Screen Time settings, set a Screen Time passcode instead.

### How do I block apps on iPhone so I can't open them?

Use Screen Time: App Limits and Downtime restrict access by time, and Content & Privacy Restrictions can limit apps outright. All of them can be undone by whoever knows the Screen Time passcode, so the passcode holder matters more than the setting.

### Is there a way to lock apps so I can't unlock them myself?

Only if someone else controls the code. Every built-in lock opens for whoever knows the passcode or owns the face. Giving a partner the Screen Time passcode, or using a mutual lock app, puts another person in the decision.
