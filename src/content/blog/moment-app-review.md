If you're asking what happened to the Moment app, here's the honest answer: the original Moment — the app that put phone-time tracking on the map before Apple ever built Screen Time into iOS — shut down in the summer of 2021 and is delisted from the App Store today. It isn't quietly still around, and any page telling you otherwise is out of date.

Before iOS had a native Screen Time feature, Moment was how people first discovered they were spending four hours a day on their phones. Then Apple built that exact feature into the OS itself, and the app that pioneered the category didn't survive what it inspired.

Here's what actually happened, and what's worth knowing if you're looking for something like it today.

---

## What the Original Moment Was

Kevin Holesh built Moment as a side project starting in December 2013, and after six rejections from Apple, launched version 1.0 in June 2014. It tracked total daily phone use and broke it down by app, sending daily and weekly summaries — the first screen time tracker on iOS, and one of the first anywhere. It got real attention fast: TechCrunch, The New York Times, the Today Show. Within a year, Holesh left freelance work to run it full time.

In 2017, Tim Kendall — Pinterest's former president — bought Moment, with Holesh staying on. The two built out a real team and office, expanding well beyond what Holesh could run solo. Over its eight-year life, Moment was downloaded roughly 9 million times, and at its peak was used by something like one in every 80 American adults.

## Why It Shut Down

Apple announced native Screen Time at WWDC 2018. In Holesh's own words, on [his account of Moment's history](https://kevinholesh.com/moment): "For the whole of Apple's customers, this was a tremendous thing and why I built Moment in the first place. But for Moment itself, it was the beginning of the end." Downloads and active customers declined from there.

By his account: "In the summer of 2021, Tim and I decided to shut Moment down. The downloads and active customers had declined and most of the team was interested in spinning off into a brand new direction." The app came down, and its old App Store listing (ID 771541926) returns nothing today — a dead link, not a live product.

The short version: Apple's own Screen Time absorbed the exact use case Moment was built for — awareness and tracking, built into the OS, free, with no separate app required. That's genuinely why Moment stopped being necessary, not a slow fade for no reason.

## A Different App With the Same Name

As of September 2026, there's a separate, unrelated app also called "Moment – Screen Time Blocker" on the App Store, built by a company called Meaningful Things GmbH, with no connection to Kevin Holesh, Tim Kendall, or the original product. It launched in late April 2026, is currently at version 1.3 (July 2026), requires iOS 26 or later, and is free with an optional roughly $14.99/year family-plan subscription. Unlike the original, it's framed as a blocker rather than a pure tracker. We haven't reviewed it and aren't endorsing it here — this is just the factual record, so you don't confuse a 2026 app with the 2014 one this page is actually about.

## The Awareness Problem

Here's the core issue with tracking as a behavior change strategy: awareness is necessary but not sufficient.

The research on self-monitoring is genuinely mixed. For some behaviors and some people, seeing the data is enough to prompt change. For others — and this appears to be the majority for phone use specifically — knowing you spent four hours on Instagram today doesn't prevent you from spending four hours on Instagram tomorrow.

This isn't a personal failure. It's a predictable outcome based on how habits work.

Phone overuse isn't driven by ignorance ("I had no idea I was using it this much"). It's driven by automatic behavior that bypasses conscious decision-making. When you open Instagram for the twentieth time today, you're not making a deliberate choice. You're following a cue-response loop that doesn't engage the part of your brain that's aware of the four-hour stat.

Information works at the deliberate reasoning level. Habits live at the automatic behavior level. These are different systems, and they don't automatically talk to each other.

## What Moment Users Actually Reported

People who used Moment tended to fall into three groups:

**Group 1: Immediate changers.** Seeing the number was genuinely shocking. They didn't realize. The awareness alone was enough to motivate them to make specific changes. This group exists and is real. It's probably 15-25% of users.

**Group 2: Aware but unchanged.** They knew exactly how much time they were spending. They saw the daily summary. The number bothered them. They opened Instagram anyway. This was the majority.

**Group 3: Trackers who disengaged.** After a few weeks, they stopped looking at the data because the notification had become background noise. The app was still installed, still tracking, still sending summaries. Nobody was home to receive them.

Moment worked well for Group 1. For Groups 2 and 3, tracking alone wasn't the intervention — and that gap is a real part of why a pure tracker, however well-designed, eventually lost out to a platform feature that did the same job for free.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What actually happened to the Moment app?

The original Moment — built by Kevin Holesh, later co-run with Tim Kendall — shut down in the summer of 2021. Holesh has said declining downloads, driven largely by Apple building Screen Time into iOS in 2018, were the main reason. It's delisted from the App Store; its old listing returns nothing today.

### Is the "Moment" app on the App Store now the same one?

No. A different, unrelated app also called "Moment – Screen Time Blocker," built by a company called Meaningful Things GmbH, launched in April 2026. It has no connection to the original team, product, or company. Don't assume reviews or history of one apply to the other.

### Does screen time tracking actually reduce phone use?

For some people, yes — particularly when the data is genuinely surprising. For people who already know they're overusing their phones, tracking adds information without adding friction, and doesn't reliably change behavior.

### What's better: Apple's built-in Screen Time or a separate tracker?

For most users, Apple Screen Time provides enough data without requiring an additional app — it's literally what replaced Moment for most people. A dedicated tracker like RescueTime is worth the subscription mainly if you want cross-device analytics beyond just phone use.

### Can I use Apple Screen Time and LockPact together?

Yes. Use Screen Time to identify which apps you're overusing and during which hours. Then set those apps as your LockPact targets for those windows. Diagnosis plus enforcement is more effective than either alone — it's the same logic that used to point people from Moment toward a locking app.
