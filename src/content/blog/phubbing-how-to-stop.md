You're mid-sentence. Your partner glances at their phone. Just for a second — a notification, probably nothing. But you notice the second, and you notice that they didn't come back to your sentence, and you notice that you've stopped talking without really deciding to.

That's phubbing. You've almost certainly done it to someone this week, and someone has almost certainly done it to you. It's common enough to have its own word and specific enough that once you know the word, you start seeing it everywhere — including in the mirror.

## What is phubbing?

Phubbing is a portmanteau of "phone" and "snubbing" — coined in May 2012 by the Australian ad agency McCann Melbourne for a Macquarie Dictionary campaign called "Stop Phubbing." The word caught on because the behavior was already everywhere; it just didn't have a name yet.

The simplest definition: phubbing is looking at your phone instead of the person you're with, in a moment where they had reason to expect your attention. Not every glance at your phone counts — checking a text while your partner is also on their phone isn't phubbing, exactly, because nobody's attention was owed to anyone in that moment. It's specifically the snub — the person right in front of you loses to the phone.

## Why does phubbing happen?

Not because you love your phone more than the person you're with. It happens because your phone requires zero effort and the person in front of you requires some.

A notification is a tiny, complete task: glance, read, done. A conversation is open-ended — you have to track what's being said, think of a response, stay present for however long it takes. Your brain, given a choice between a task with a clear finish line and one without, will often reach for the finish line. That's not a character flaw. It's just what phones are built to exploit, and they're built by people whose job is making that exploit work.

There's also a social-mirroring piece. Once one person in a conversation picks up their phone, it gives the other person unspoken permission to do the same — even if they hadn't been about to. Boredom in a lull, anxiety about a notification, plain habit: any of these can start it, and once it starts, phubbing tends to spread through a room rather than stay contained to one person.

## Is phubbing actually bad for relationships?

Yes, and there's real research behind it, not just a strong word for something mildly annoying. A widely cited 2016 study by James Roberts and Meredith David at Baylor University — "My Life Has Become a Major Distraction From My Cell Phone: Partner Phubbing and Relationship Satisfaction Among Romantic Partners," published in *Computers in Human Behavior* — found that partner phubbing predicted lower relationship satisfaction, which in turn predicted lower personal well-being and higher depression. Later research (including dyadic studies looking at both partners at once) has generally reinforced the finding: it's not really about how much time you spend on your phone in general, it's specifically about being phubbed *by the person you're closest to*, in the moments meant for them.

The mechanism makes sense once you sit with it. Being phubbed doesn't just mean losing a conversation — it signals, even unintentionally, that whatever's on the screen ranked higher in that moment. Nobody means to send that message. The phone sends it anyway.

## How do I know if I'm the one phubbing?

The honest test: think back to your last three meals with your partner, or your last few minutes in bed before sleep. Did your phone come out? Did you finish reading whatever it was before you looked up, or did you keep scrolling past the point where the original notification was handled?

Most people who ask "am I a phubber" already suspect the answer. The more useful question is *when* — not "do I use my phone too much" in the abstract, but which specific moments (dinner, the first ten minutes home from work, the last twenty minutes before sleep) are the ones where it costs you something. Those are the moments worth targeting, not your phone use as a whole.

## How do you stop phubbing your partner?

Start smaller than "I'm going to use my phone less." That goal is too vague to act on and too easy to fail at by Tuesday. Instead:

**Name the specific windows, not the general behavior.** "I won't touch my phone during dinner" is a rule you can actually keep. "I'll use my phone less" is a feeling, not a rule.

**Put the phone somewhere that isn't the table.** Visibility matters more than people expect — a phone face-down on the table still pulls attention every time it lights up. In another room, or at least in a bag, changes the behavior more than willpower does.

**Notice the mirror effect and use it on purpose.** If your partner reaching for their phone makes you reach for yours, the reverse is also true: you putting yours away first makes it easier for them to do the same, without either of you saying anything about it.

**Expect the itch, and expect it to pass.** The first few times you sit through a phone-free window, you'll feel the pull to check — that's normal, not a sign you're failing. It fades within days, not weeks, once your hands stop expecting the phone to be an option.

## How do you tell your partner they're phubbing you without starting a fight?

This is the part people get wrong most often, and it's worth doing carefully because the wrong opener turns a fixable problem into a fight about something else entirely.

"You're always on your phone" is an accusation dressed as an observation, and your partner will hear the accusation, not the observation. It puts them on the defensive, and the conversation becomes about whether "always" is fair instead of about what you actually need.

Try naming the moment and the feeling instead of the general pattern: "When we're watching something together and you check your phone, I feel like I've lost you mid-scene, and I end up not wanting to bring things up anymore." That's specific, it's about a feeling rather than a verdict on their character, and it doesn't require them to defend their overall relationship with their phone — just to hear what one moment does to you. ## Is phubbing normal, or is it a sign something's wrong?

Both, depending on how much and where. Nearly everyone phubs occasionally — a glance at a text mid-conversation isn't a crisis, and treating every phone check as a betrayal will exhaust you faster than the phubbing itself does. What's worth addressing is a pattern: the same windows, night after night, where the phone reliably wins. That's not a moral failing either — it's a habit loop, and habit loops respond to structure, not guilt.

## What actually stops phubbing, long-term?

Individual willpower is the weakest lever here, for the same reason solo screen-time limits tend to fail: the person who's tempted to check their phone is also the person who decides whether the rule applies right now. "I won't check my phone at dinner" is easy to override at 7:42pm when you're actually at dinner and genuinely bored.

What works better is making the boundary mutual and a little bit externally enforced — both people agree to the same window, and there's some friction (a partner who notices, a lock that requires asking) between wanting the phone and getting it. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does phubbing mean?

Phubbing — a blend of "phone" and "snubbing" — means ignoring the person you're with in favor of your phone, in a moment where your attention was reasonably expected. It was coined in 2012 by Australian ad agency McCann Melbourne for a Macquarie Dictionary "Stop Phubbing" campaign.

### Is phubbing bad for relationships?

Research on "partner phubbing," notably a 2016 Baylor University study published in *Computers in Human Behavior*, found it predicts lower relationship satisfaction, which in turn is linked to lower personal well-being and higher depression. It's specifically being phubbed by a close partner — not general phone use — that the research connects to relationship harm.

### How do I stop phubbing my partner?

Target specific windows (dinner, bedtime, the first ten minutes home) rather than "using my phone less" in general. Put the phone out of reach, not just face-down on the table, and expect the urge to check it to fade within days once the habit breaks.

### How do I tell my partner they're phubbing me?

Name a specific moment and how it made you feel, instead of a general accusation like "you're always on your phone." Specificity keeps the conversation about the moment, not a referendum on their whole relationship with their phone.

### Is occasional phubbing normal?

Yes — nearly everyone does it sometimes, and that's not a crisis on its own. What's worth addressing is a repeated pattern in the same moments, since that's a habit loop rather than a one-off distraction.
